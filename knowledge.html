Jade KnowledgeNotes & Basics

The things you hear most often, written up in plain language. First, one key fact: jadeite (feicui) and Hetian jade (nephrite) are two completely different minerals. Jadeite is a pyroxene; nephrite is made of the amphibole tremolite.

Jadeite Hetian Jade Glossary Care

JadeiteFeicui

The trade judges jadeite by three things: texture (zhong), transparency (shui) and color (se) — plus craftsmanship and flaws such as cracks, cotton and inclusions. The three are linked: a fine texture usually brings better transparency, and makes the color look more alive.

Texture — the structure

Zhong describes how fine the crystal grains are and how tightly they are packed. The finer and denser the grains, the "older" and better the texture: it feels smoother, shines more and stays stable over time.

Glass typeGrains so fine they can't be seen with the naked eye; transparent like glass. Strong glassy luster and often a floating glow. Very rare.
Icy typeA little less transparent than glass type — clear like ice, sometimes with a few wisps of cotton. Graded as high-icy or icy. Pale-colored pieces are called "icy white" or "clear-water".
Glutinous typeTranslucent, like cooked glutinous-rice soup: smooth, soft and mellow, with no visible grains. Between icy and glutinous is called "glutinous-icy".
Bean typeCoarse grains you can see, like beans; lower transparency and weaker luster. The most common and affordable grade — often green ("bean green").

Tip: shine a flashlight through it and see whether the light spreads evenly, and whether you can see graininess or "fly wings" (flashes from crystal cleavage surfaces). Both help you judge how fine the texture is.

Transparency — the "water"

Shui ("water") is how transparent jadeite is. Traditionally it's measured in "fen": light reaching about 3 mm inside is "one-fen water", 6 mm is "two-fen", 9 mm or more is "three-fen". Good water makes a piece look alive; poor water looks "dry" and dull.

  • How to look: hold a small flashlight against the back or side and see how deep the light goes and how bright the glow is.
  • Mind the thickness: the thinner the piece, the clearer it looks — so be skeptical of the "water" in thin slices and pierced carvings.

Color

Color is judged on four qualities, pure, bright, rich and even:

  • Pure: a true hue, not leaning yellow, blue or gray.
  • Bright: vivid and lively, not dark.
  • Rich: saturated and deep — but not so dark it looks black.
  • Even: spread evenly, without patches or obvious changes.

Color also has to suit the base: a lovely color on a clean, clear base is when "color and water set each other off".

Glowing under UV can suggest Type B, but some natural jadeite fluoresces weakly and some treated stones don't — so fluorescence alone proves nothing.

Common colors

Imperial green

An intense, pure, vivid emerald green on a glass or high-icy base — the very top of jadeite color. Extremely rare and extremely expensive.

Vivid green

A bright, lively green with a slightly yellow tint — it looks "sunny". Lighter than imperial green and a much-loved true green.

Clear-water green

A faint green spread evenly through a transparent base, like a lake on a clear day. Fresh and elegant; needs even color and a clean base.

Lavender

Also called "spring color": pink-, eggplant- or blue-toned violet. Usually found in glutinous or bean textures; strong lavender with fine texture is rare.

Yellow & red jadeite

Yellow and reddish-brown colors formed by iron staining near the outer skin of the rough. The character "fei" originally meant red. Bright, translucent pieces are valuable.

Icy white

Colorless or nearly colorless icy jadeite: clean and clear, prized for its floating glow. Very popular with younger buyers.

Black jadeite

Looks black in reflected light, but shines deep green through thin edges under a strong light. Fine-grained pieces are often carved into Guanyin or Buddha figures.

Oily green

A darker green with gray or blue tones and an oily luster. Moderately priced — a good-texture oily green can be very easy to love.

Hetian JadeNephrite

Hetian jade is mostly tremolite. Its interlocking fibrous structure makes it extremely tough, and it has a soft, oily luster — a "warm" beauty, as opposed to jadeite's "clear" beauty.

Key fact: under the Chinese national standard, "Hetian jade" is a mineral name, not a place of origin. Any tremolite jade — from Xinjiang, Qinghai, Russia or Korea — can be sold as Hetian jade. Origin makes a huge difference to feel and price, so if you want Xinjiang material, ask clearly.

There is also Gobi material: jade exposed in the desert and polished by wind and sand for ages. It often has tiny wind-etched pits ("sweat pores"), a dense texture and a good oily feel.

Some sellers tumble mountain material into pebble shapes and dye a "fake skin" to pass it off as seed material. Real skin color soaks into the jade, fades gradually and sits in cracks and hollows; fake skin sits on the surface and looks unnatural. For valuable seed material, ask for a certificate and buy from someone you trust.

Common colors

White jade

White Hetian jade. The finest — silky, oily and white like congealed fat — is called "mutton-fat white", the top grade of all nephrite.

Greenish-white jade

Between white and celadon: white with a green-gray hint. Plentiful and good value — used for many carvings.

Celadon jade

Pale to deep gray-green. Abundant and available in large blocks, so often used for big carvings.

Green jade

Spinach-green to dark green. The purer and more even the color, and the fewer black specks, the better. Popular for bangles and bead bracelets.

Black jade

Black or black-and-white, colored by graphite. Graded as fully black, clustered or dotted; an even full black is prized. Unlike dark green jade, it doesn't show green under a strong light.

Sugar jade

Yellow-brown to red-brown, from iron seeping in along cracks — like brown sugar. Often occurs with white or greenish-white jade, great for color-play carving.

Yellow jade

Pale yellow to honey yellow. Fine, well-colored yellow nephrite is very rare and valuable. (Not to be confused with the gemstone topaz.)

Smoky violet

Gray-violet, lotus-root tones, mostly from Qinghai. An unusual color with a lot of charm.

Where it comes from

Xinjiang

Along the Kunlun and Altun mountains — Hotan, Qiemo, Ruoqiang. Fine and oily; traditionally the most prized and most expensive. The Manas area north of the Tianshan is known for green jade.

Qinghai

Around Golmud. More translucent and "watery", usually less oily than Xinjiang material, often with "water lines" (clear streaks). Has distinctive smoky-violet and emerald-celadon colors.

Russia

Mainly the Lake Baikal region. Usually very white but drier and less oily, often with sugar color. The Sayan Mountains also produce fine green jade.

Korea

From Chuncheon. Often yellowish or grayish, coarser and less oily — the most affordable.

Others

Canadian green jade (bright green, plentiful), and "river-ground jade" from Xiuyan in Liaoning (also tremolite).

Care TipsKeeping It Beautiful

🫧 Avoid knocks

Jadeite is hard (Mohs 6.5–7) but can break on impact; nephrite is tough but can still chip. Take it off for sports, heavy lifting, housework and showers — and watch bangles around door frames and table edges.

🧴 Keep away from chemicals

Perfume, dish soap, bleach and acidic or alkaline cleaners dull the surface and wax. Treated Type B pieces suffer most — the resin ages faster.

🔥 No heat or sudden temperature change

Keep it away from flames, radiators and saunas, and out of long direct sun. Sudden temperature changes can widen existing small cracks.

🧼 Clean gently

Warm water, a soft brush and a soft cloth to dry. Avoid ultrasonic cleaners for cracked or set pieces.

📦 Store separately

Keep each piece in its own soft pouch or a divided box so it doesn't scratch — or get scratched by — diamonds and other stones.

🤲 Wear your nephrite

Hetian jade gets more lustrous the more you wear and handle it. No need to oil it — oil only gives a temporary fake shine and attracts dust.

🛏️ Bangles off at night

It's easy to knock or crush a bangle in your sleep. Taking it off is safer.

🔍 Check regularly

For set pieces, check that the prongs are tight and the cord isn't worn, and replace them in time.
